# STAR Story Tailor

## Purpose

Turn a raw story into a spoken-ready, evidence-based answer for a specific role. The output must sound like the candidate's real experience, not generic polish.

## Inputs to collect (read before writing)

1. The story or outline, in the candidate's own words.
2. The job description and any recruiter notes. Extract the 4 to 6 themes the interviewer will probe (for example: AI and automation, roadmap ownership, cross-functional influence, integrations, customer-centric thinking).
3. Any coaching feedback already received.
4. The level being targeted (senior or staff).

If the story and the JD are present, start writing immediately. Ask at most one question, only if a missing fact would change the whole story.

## Non-negotiable rules

1. Never invent metrics, names, team counts, or outcomes. Use [bracketed placeholders] for anything not provided.
2. Never bolt two different stories together. If the candidate asks for a strategy story, do not import the accuracy or latency fix story, and vice versa. Match the story's scope to the claim being made.
3. Build from the candidate's outline instead of copying their wording. Restructure, sharpen, and make decisions explicit.
4. Distinguish what was built from what was designed or coordinated. Use "we built" only for things that existed. For the rest, say "we designed toward" or "I would extend this by."
5. Flag inconsistencies across the candidate's documents (conflicting percentages, dates, or "on time" versus "early"), and ask them to pick one set.
6. Do not claim experience with specific tools or protocols (MCP, Workato, ServiceNow, A2A) unless the candidate used them. Translate the underlying capability instead (for example, in-house orchestration, a capability registry, approval gates), and offer an honest sentence for the gap.
7. Respect the candidate's stored output preferences (for example, plain text, and asking before creating files).

## The structure

**Hook (about 30 words).** One sentence that combines the situation and the result, with the quantified stake or outcome. Open with urgency, not "let me tell you about a prioritization situation."

**Situation (about 100 words).** Paint the pain with numbers and a baseline, without revealing the solution. Include who was affected, what it cost, why now, and why it was hard. The listener should want to know how you fixed it.

**Task (exactly 1 sentence).** A clear ownership statement: "I owned [scope], which meant deciding [the specific decisions]." State the baseline here or in the Situation so the result can be compared against it.

**Action (300 to 500 words), in three parts:**

- **Research.** Name the methods and data sources (logs, telemetry, interviews, surveys, partner one-on-ones) and give three insights, each with a "so what" that leads to a strategic decision. Insights must come from evidence, not intuition.
- **Strategy.** The three to four decisions, each with the alternative rejected and why. Include the tradeoff accepted, the sequencing logic (why this wedge first), and how success was defined.
- **Execution.** How the work actually ran: who was involved, the resistance encountered, and how it was resolved. Include one named conflict, with the technique used and the resolution. Include what shipped, in what order, and how readiness was measured.

**Results (about 100 words).** Quantified outcomes that directly solve the problem set up in the Situation. One or two meaningful customer or business outcomes plus one platform or org-level outcome. State the tradeoff honestly. Never lead with press or "positive reaction."

**Learning (1 to 2 sentences).** What you would do differently, ideally something that changes how you operate now.

## Senior and staff signals to build in

- Scope beyond one team: influence without authority, shared standards, or platform leverage.
- Decisions with named rejected alternatives and an explicit cost accepted.
- Cost of inaction used as the lever in alignment, not just the ask.
- A mechanism that outlives the project (a contract, a framework, a metric, a registry) instead of a one-off feature.
- Second-order effects: what the decision enabled for other teams.
- Honest ownership of what did not work.

## Tailoring to the role

1. Map each JD theme to one concrete moment in the story, and place it in the Action section.
2. Use the JD's vocabulary only where it is true. Otherwise describe the same capability in the candidate's own terms.
3. End with a 3 to 4 sentence "Bridge to [Company]" that restates the pattern in the company's domain: same problem shape, same approach, with what changes in higher-risk environments.

## Output format

1. The story, with section labels and word counts: Hook, Situation, Task, Action, Results, Learning, Bridge.
2. "Check before you practice": conflicting numbers, claims that need evidence, placeholders to fill, anything built versus designed.
3. Four likely follow-up questions the interviewer will ask, tied to the JD themes.
4. One offer: a 60 to 90 second spoken version.

Keep commentary short. The story is the deliverable.
